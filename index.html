      OUR WORK

      The challenge and how we address it

      The challenge

      We are taking part in the official NASA Space Apps 2026 challenge Identify Earth Locations that Analog the Permanent Moon Base Locations and Mars. The challenge asks teams to use open data on the Earth, the Moon and Mars, from NASA and other agencies, to identify and characterize places on Earth that can serve as analogs of future lunar bases or Mars landing sites. It also asks teams to explain what makes a place an analog and where the analogy breaks down, because no place on Earth fully reproduces another planet.

      Our solution

      1. Sites on the Moon and Mars. For a given crew and mission objective, the app ranks candidate sites. Terrain, ice, temperature and resources are computed from measured NASA PDS layers (LOLA, Diviner, Lunar Prospector, MOLA, TES, GRS). Illumination, radiation, communications link and science value are demonstration estimates, and the weights of each objective were set by the team. Each site sheet says so.
      2. Measured Earth analogs. A scientific engine written in Python compares the relief of the lunar or Martian region with every window on Earth of the same physical size. It returns the candidates on the map, with their uncertainty, their sources and their limitations. It uses no machine learning, no subjective weights and no similarity percentages.
      3. Earth context. For each training site, the NASA Earthdata catalogs are queried live: clear sky (HLS), relief (ASTER GDEM) and temperature (MODIS).

      Which algorithms we use, and what for

      AlgorithmPurpose
      Azimuthal equidistant and polar stereographic projections (Snyder 1987)Cut windows of the same physical size in km on all three bodies, and read the LOLA polar DEMs with their official formula.
      Area-mean resamplingBring every DEM to the same cell size h without creating detail that does not exist: h is never smaller than that of the coarsest product.
      Least-squares plane per windowRemove differences in vertical reference (lunar sphere, areoid, terrestrial geoid) before comparing.
      Evans–Young polynomial on a 3×3 neighborhood (Evans 1979; Florinsky 2016)Slope, roughness, and profile, plan and mean curvature.
      Masked FFT variogram (Marcotte 1996)How relief is distributed across scales, from h to L/2: two terrains with the same histogram can be organized differently.
      Moran's I (Moran 1950)Whether steep slopes cluster (scarps, crater rims) or are scattered.
      Wasserstein-1 distance (Vallender 1974)Difference between distributions of relative elevation, slope, roughness and curvatures, in their own units.
      Kolmogorov–Smirnov with effective sample size (Stephens 1970; Bretherton et al. 1999)Test of distributions that does not treat neighboring pixels, which are correlated, as independent observations.
      Energy distance (Székely and Rizzo 2013)Compare the joint distribution of the two curvatures, which share a unit (1/km); also used as an alternative metric in validation.
      Percentiles, Pareto dominance and leximax ordering (Ehrgott 2005)Rank candidates on seven criteria without subjective weights.
      Block bootstrap (Künsch 1989) and Kendall's τ (Kendall 1938)Measure the uncertainty and stability of the ranking under changes in resolution, window, noise, gaps, normalization, metric and vertical reference.

      Data

      All data come from the official challenge resources. Each result includes the citation and the DOI or URL of every source used.

      1. Smith, D. E. et al. (2010). The Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter investigation on the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter mission. Space Science Reviews, 150, 209–241. doi:10.1007/s11214-009-9512-y. Data: LRO-L-LOLA-4-GDR-V1.0 (LDEM_16, LDEM_75S/N_240M), NASA PDS Geosciences Node.
      2. Smith, D. E. et al. (2001). Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter: Experiment summary after the first year of global mapping of Mars. J. Geophys. Res., 106(E10), 23689–23722. doi:10.1029/2000JE001364. Data: MGS-M-MOLA-5-MEGDR-L3-V1.0, NASA PDS.
      3. NASA JPL (2013). NASA Shuttle Radar Topography Mission Global 30 arc second V002. NASA LP DAAC. doi:10.5067/MEASURES/SRTM/SRTMGL30.002.
      4. Williams, J.-P. et al. (2017), Diviner; Feldman, W. C. et al. (2001) and Lawrence, D. J. et al. (2002), Lunar Prospector; Boynton, W. V. et al. (2007), Odyssey GRS; Putzig, N. E. and Mellon, M. T. (2007), TES. Full citations in each globe layer.
      5. NASA Earthdata (HLS v2.0, ASTER GDEM v3, MODIS MOD11A1 v061): citations and DOIs in the panel of each training site.

      What we do not claim

      • That a place on Earth reproduces the Moon or Mars: gravity, vacuum or atmosphere, radiation and temperatures are not reproduced.
      • That similar relief means the same geological origin or the same suitability for a mission. We keep geomorphological similarity, environmental similarity and suitability separate.
      • Anything below the scale of the global data (≈ 1–4 km): rocks, regolith and caves are out of scope.
